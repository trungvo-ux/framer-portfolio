Rebuilding checkout to earn trust at the moment of payment.

Client
Ledger (fintech)
Role
Lead Product Designer
Timeline
12 weeks · 2025
Team
2 engineers, 1 PM, 1 researcher

Overview

Ledger's checkout converted well on paper and poorly in practice: users started payment, hesitated, and left. Drop-off concentrated on the final step — the one asking for money. Our hypothesis was that the problem wasn't friction, it was doubt.

Over twelve weeks we re-designed the flow around a single principle: every screen must answer "what happens next, and can I undo it?" before it asks for anything.

The problem

Session recordings showed a distinctive pattern: at the payment step, users scrolled up and down repeatedly, re-checking the amount, the fee, and the recipient. The interface answered each question on a different screen, so re-checking meant going backwards — and going backwards cleared form state.

  • 41% of started checkouts were abandoned at the payment step.
  • Fees were revealed only on the confirmation screen.
  • "Back" lost user input, punishing the act of double-checking.
  • Support tickets clustered around "was I charged twice?"

Research

We ran nine moderated sessions and a diary study with recent churners. The most useful finding was emotional, not mechanical: people described the old flow as a hallway of closed doors — each step hid what the next one would ask.

"I just want to see the whole bill before I put my card number in. Is that weird?" — Participant 06, diary study

Design principles

Three rules shaped every screen that followed:

  • Total cost first. Amount, fee, and arrival time visible before any input is requested.
  • Checking is free. All steps live on one page; editing one never resets another.
  • Confirmation is a receipt. The final screen mirrors the summary the user already approved — no new information at the moment of commitment.

Outcome

We shipped behind a 50/50 experiment and held it for six weeks — long enough to cover two billing cycles and rule out novelty effects.

−38%

abandonment at the payment step

+19%

completed checkouts overall

−54%

"was I charged?" support tickets

The quieter result mattered most: median time on the payment step went up by eleven seconds. Users weren't rushing through — they were reading, deciding, and staying.
